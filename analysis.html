Spatial & Cultural Analysis

Temporary Architecture through Indian Knowledge Systems

Temporary architecture in Delhi operates as a dynamic cultural and epistemic system rather than a fixed or autonomous built form. These structures emerge in response to specific social, religious, and political events, transforming urban space through collective participation, design intensity, and temporal concentration. Their significance does not lie in permanence but in their ability to reconfigure everyday urban environments into heightened experiential zones within limited durations. This understanding aligns closely with Indian Knowledge Systems, where space is not conceived as static matter but as something continuously produced through action, ritual, and perception. In Buddhist philosophy, the principle of impermanence (anicca) establishes that all conditioned phenomena are transient, existing only within cycles of formation and dissolution (Buddha, Dhammapada). Applied to urban space, this suggests that architectural forms are not fixed entities but temporary configurations within ongoing processes of social and cultural becoming.

Production of Space and Ritual Activation

Temporary structures in Delhi demonstrate that space is continuously produced through cultural activity, echoing the epistemological framework of Henri Lefebvre, who argues that space is socially produced rather than pre-given. In the context of Indian urbanism, this production is intensified through ritual and event-based practices. Streets become ritual pathways during festivals,open grounds transform into sacred or symbolic environments, and everyday public sites evolve into spaces of congregation, resistance, and celebration. Durga Puja pandals, Ramlila grounds, and temporary protest stages exemplify this transformation, where spatial meaning is activated through collective occupation rather than architectural permanence. Within Indian Knowledge Systems, this resonates with classical aesthetic theory in the Natyashastra, attributed to Bharata Muni, where space is not an inert container but a performative field activated through enactment. The concept of rasa suggests that meaning arises only through lived experience and dissolves once the performance concludes. Similarly, temporary architecture exists not as an object but as an event-space, activated through participation and deactivated through its conclusion.

Urban space in Delhi is not static—it is repeatedly constructed through cultural practice.

Performance, Temporality, and Experiential Intensity

Temporary architectural forms in Delhi function as performative environments where meaning is generated through movement, sensory engagement, and visual density. Their short lifespan intensifies cultural experience, producing what may be described as compressed spatial temporality—where design, ritual, and social interaction converge within limited timeframes. This aligns with the work of Richard Schechner, who conceptualises performance as “restored behavior,” emphasising repetition, transformation, and embodied temporality. In Delhi’s festival landscapes, architecture itself becomes performative: structures are not merely backgrounds for events but active participants in shaping ritual experience. This performative logic is further deepened in the aesthetic philosophy of Abhinavagupta, who argues that aesthetic experience (rasa-svāda) exists only in the immediacy of perception. Once the moment passes, it cannot be fully retrieved except as memory. Thus, the architectural environment is meaningful only during its activation, reinforcing the idea that temporality is central to spatial significance.

Production, Disappearance, and Cultural Memory

Even after temporary structures are dismantled, they persist through memory, visual documentation, and collective recollection. This suggests that cultural meaning in urban environments is not dependent on material continuity but on experiential and mnemonic traces. From the perspective of Indian Knowledge Systems, this aligns with a cyclical understanding of time, where creation and dissolution are part of the same continuum rather than opposing states. Ritual architectures—such as Durga Puja pandals—are deliberately constructed with the awareness of their own disappearance, reflecting a worldview in which completion is achieved through dissolution rather than preservation. This logic is reinforced by Ananda Coomaraswamy, who emphasises that traditional Indian art is inseparable from ritual action and cosmic order. In this framework, the value of a structure lies not in its endurance but in its participation within a larger temporal and spiritual process. Even in contemporary urban contexts, this principle persists: temporary architecture in Delhi leaves behind not physical permanence but layers of cultural memory, mediated through photographs, digital circulation, and embodied recollection.
